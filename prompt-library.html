Prompt library

Prompts you paste into a chat

Each step has one prompt that runs the whole step. That step prompt already carries out every method inside the step; you do not assemble it from parts. The methods are listed under each step so you can see what the step prompt does, and each one is also available as its own small prompt for the times you want to run just that move in a chat. To run the whole method across every task without pasting anything, use the system prompt.

01

Frame the topic

Turn a topic into a question you can actually research, before any searching starts.

Prompt to run this complete step
Prompt
Work through the framing of this research with me before searching.

Use the topic we have been discussing, or ask me for it if there is none yet.
If a later step sent us back here because the evidence broke the original
framing, do not start over: say what specifically changed, and reframe only what
has to change.

1. Restate it as the decision it informs, the questions needed to make that
   decision, and the evidence that would answer them.
2. Tell me whether the question as posed is the right one, and whether a nearby
   question would be more worth researching.
3. Once we agree the question, mark which parts are background and which the
   conclusion will depend on, so we know where to go deep.

Wait for me to confirm the framing before moving on. Once I confirm, end your
reply with a short block I can carry into the next step:

  CARRY FORWARD
  Decision this serves: [one line]
  Settled question: [one line]
  Load-bearing (verify hard): [list]
  Background (report but do not rely on): [list]
Sub-prompts for each research method within this step
Restate the question
Rewrite the topic three ways: the decision it informs, the questions you would need to answer to make that decision, and the evidence that would answer them. If a topic cannot be rewritten like this, it is not yet ready to research.
Run alone
Check the framing
Say what nearby or better question the topic points to, and what the version as asked might miss.
no separate prompt
Place the effort
Mark which lines of enquiry are just background and which the conclusion will hang on, so the deep work goes where it matters.
no separate prompt
02

Gather and verify

Collect material and work out what can be trusted, one item at a time.

Prompt to run this complete step
Prompt
Research this question, working to these rules the whole way through.

Start by restating what you are inheriting from the framing step: the decision,
the settled question, and the load-bearing versus background split. Work from
that. If it is not present in the chat, ask for it rather than inventing scope.
If your research gives you reason to change any of it, say so explicitly instead
of quietly re-scoping.

- As you read, flag leads worth following: named people, referenced but unlinked
  documents, conflicting numbers, and things that should be present and are
  missing.
- Treat weak sources (marketing, social posts, unattributed articles) as leads
  only. Take the checkable detail out and confirm it against a primary document,
  official body, peer-reviewed work, or named specialist reporting. Do not
  accept another version of the same weak source repeating the claim as
  confirmation.
- Record every claim that matters in a table: the claim, whether the conclusion
  depends on it, its status, its best source and what kind it is, and what you
  searched.
- Do not rest anything important on a single source when a second could be
  found. After three failed searches for a detail, mark it unconfirmed and move
  on.
- If what you find undoes something the framing assumed, or makes the settled
  question the wrong one to be asking, stop and say so. Recommend going back to
  the framing step rather than researching on against a brief the evidence has
  already broken.

Show me the table as you build it, and tell me what you could not confirm.

End with a short process note:

  PROCESS NOTE
  Inherited: [the decision and load-bearing split you worked from]
  Did with it: [verified which load-bearing claims; what you left as background]
  Drift: [none, or what changed; if it breaks the framing, say to go back to framing]
  Handing on: [the confirmed claims and the still-open ones the next step needs]
Sub-prompts for each research method within this step
Spot the lead
Some details are always worth a follow-up, even in a poor source: a named person moving between organisations, a filing mentioned but not linked, a number that clashes with one you already have, and things that should be present and are missing. Missing things matter most and are the easiest to overlook.
Run alone
Chase the detail
Pull the checkable specifics out of a weak source, the names, dates, figures and references, and follow them to a source that can back them up. A weak source is never evidence; it is a supply of things to check.
Run alone
Resolve and record claims
Every detail you chase ends in one of three states, written down: confirmed, contradicted, or unconfirmed and flagged. Those three endings prevent the two quiet failures, a weak claim slipping through as fact, and a real lead getting dropped because its first source was poor.
Run alone
Know when to stop
Set a floor and a ceiling. Nothing the conclusion depends on should rest on a single source when a second could be found. Once three searches for a detail have failed, mark it unconfirmed and move on.
no separate prompt
03

Analyse

Produce findings that are not sitting in any single source.

Prompt to run this complete step
Prompt
Help me analyse the material we have, rather than summarise it. I want findings
that are not obvious from any single source.

Start by restating what you are inheriting: the settled question and the
confirmed material the gathering step handed on. Analyse only what was
confirmed; do not revive claims that failed verification. If something you need
is missing, ask rather than filling the gap with unverified material.

- Where two things can be set against each other (a policy against a lifecycle,
  one option against another), lay them out and read the shape of the result:
  where things spread, bunch, crowd or leave gaps, and what that implies.
- Where the two are peers, tell me where they differ: what each treats as
  central, where one is specific and the other vague.
- If a comparison does not hold together, say so and suggest a better one rather
  than forcing it. If the comparisons keep failing to hold, treat that as a sign
  the question was framed wrongly and recommend returning to the framing step,
  rather than forcing findings out of material that will not support them.

For each finding, tell me what in the material supports it.

End with a short process note:

  PROCESS NOTE
  Inherited: [the question and the confirmed material you worked from]
  Did with it: [what you compared, what shape you read]
  Drift: [none, or what changed; if comparisons will not hold, say to go back to framing]
  Handing on: [the findings the conclusion should be built from]
Sub-prompts for each research method within this step
Put two patterns side by side
Lay one thing against another in a table, then read the shape of the result, not the cells. Obligations spread across every stage though they came from one statement point to a coordination problem; obligations bunched into an easy group and a hard group point to a feasibility problem. Name the shape you want, spread, clustering, concentration, gaps, or the AI just reports each cell.
Run alone
Read the differences
When the two things are peers, the finding is where they differ: where one is specific and the other vague, how they divide the subject up, what each treats as central.
no separate prompt
Check the fit
Sometimes most of one thing will not line up against the other. Then the finding is that the two do not belong together. Say so and suggest a better pairing rather than forcing the comparison.
no separate prompt
04

Conclude

Test the finding and put it in a form you can trust.

Prompt to run this complete step
Prompt
We are ready to conclude. Work from the research in this chat, and work through
these in order.

Start by restating the whole chain you are inheriting: the decision, the settled
question, the confirmed claims, and the findings from analysis. Build the
conclusion only from confirmed material and only in service of the stated
decision. If the conclusion you reach does not actually serve that decision, say
so rather than answering a different question.

1. State what the verified material actually supports. Not the direction most of
   the material seems to point, but what has actually been confirmed.
2. Try to disprove that conclusion. Search for evidence against it, worded to
   find the opposite. Address the strongest objection you find.
3. Write the conclusion so it names the specific claims it rests on, each
   confirmed.
4. Check the result: every fact traceable to a good source, anything unconfirmed
   marked as such, the contrary case addressed.
5. If a claim the conclusion needs fails its check, or the contrary evidence
   shifts a claim others were built on, do not conclude over it. Say which
   earlier step has to be reopened (the gathering of that claim, or the framing
   if the whole question is affected) and stop there.

Show me the conclusion and the claims it depends on.

End with a short process note:

  PROCESS NOTE
  Inherited: [the decision, question, and confirmed claims you built from]
  Did with it: [how the conclusion follows from them]
  Drift: [none, or where it departs; if a claim failed, say which step to reopen]
  Rests on: [the specific confirmed claims the conclusion depends on]
Sub-prompts for each research method within this step
Look for evidence against the conclusion
Search for evidence the conclusion is wrong, not more that it is right. Word the search to find the opposite, then deal with the strongest objection you find even when it does not change the answer. "Consider other views" produces a hedge; searching for the opposite produces new information.
Run alone
Build the finding
State what the checked material actually supports, which is not always the direction most of the material seems to point.
no separate prompt
Show what it rests on
The conclusion names the specific claims it depends on, and each of those has been confirmed.
no separate prompt
Test the draft
Check the finished piece against the standards: every fact traced to a good source, anything unconfirmed marked rather than asserted or dropped, the contrary case addressed. It catches problems even on work written without the method, though it cannot do the checking that never happened.
Run alone
05

Report

Turn the concluded research into the layered post people will actually read.

Prompt to run this complete step
Prompt
Turn the concluded research into a report for internal sharing. Work from the
research in this chat, or ask me where it is.

Start by restating what you are inheriting: the decision this served, the
confirmed conclusion, and the claims it rests on. Report only what was
confirmed. Anything unconfirmed is named as such or left out, not smoothed into
the prose.

Produce three layers, each doing only its own job.

1. Top post. The specific finding, stated as a fact, and one sentence on why it
   matters to the people who will read it. Name the actual development, not a
   category. Nothing else goes here.
2. Thread. The questions a reader has right after the finding: why now, what has
   actually changed, who should care, and any flag. Short paragraphs, the
   load-bearing word first. One comparison or piece of context if it earns its
   place. Do not walk through the source material in order.
3. Linked document. The full treatment, for the reader who wants depth: the
   finding stated first, then the evidence with its sourcing and how well each
   claim is stood up, the second-order implications, and the limits. Front-load
   the conclusion within each section rather than building up to it.

Keep the finding the same across all three layers; the layers differ in depth,
not in what they claim. State the conclusion first, never buried in a closing
line. Plain declarative sentences.

End with a short process note:

  PROCESS NOTE
  Inherited: [the decision, conclusion, and confirmed claims you reported from]
  Did with it: [how the finding is carried across the three layers]
  Drift: [none, or where the report departs from the conclusion and why]
  Rests on: [the confirmed claims the report presents as fact]
Sub-prompts for each research method within this step
Write the top post
The specific finding stated as a fact, and one sentence on why it matters to the reader. The actual development, not a category label. Nothing else.
Run alone
Write the thread
The questions a reader has right after the finding: why now, what changed, who should care. Short paragraphs, the load-bearing word first.
Run alone
Write the linked document
The full treatment for the reader who wants depth: finding first, then the evidence with its sourcing and how well each claim stands up, the implications, and the limits.
Run alone
Hold the layers apart
Each layer does only its own job. Content that leaks up or down the chain breaks the structure, so the top post does not do the thread's work and the thread does not do the document's.
no separate prompt

Restate the question

What it does
Turns a loose topic into a question you can actually research.
What it replicates
A researcher rarely takes a topic at face value. They work out what decision it serves and what a useful answer would look like, and often find the real question sits just to one side of the one they were handed.
When to use it
At the start of a piece of work, or partway through when you realise the question was never properly pinned down.
Prompt
Help me turn what I am researching into a properly researchable question.

If we have already been discussing a topic in this chat, work from that. If not,
ask me for the topic.

Restate it as three things:
1. The decision this research is meant to inform.
2. The questions that would have to be answered to make that decision.
3. The kind of evidence that would answer each of those questions.

If it cannot be restated this way, tell me why and suggest a version that can.
Also tell me if there is a nearby question that looks more worth researching
than the one on the table, and why.

Wait for me to confirm the question before searching.
Run just this method / Gather

Spot the lead

What it does
Points out the details worth following up, including the ones that are easy to miss.
What it replicates
An experienced researcher notices things a casual reader skims past: a name, a referenced-but-unlinked document, a number that does not match another, or something that should be present and simply is not.
When to use it
On material already in the chat, or new material you are about to bring in.
Prompt
Look at the material for leads, the way a researcher would rather than a
summariser.

Use whatever material is already in this chat. If there is none, or you want me
to point at something specific, ask.

Point out every detail worth following up, including:
- a named person, especially one moving between roles or organisations
- a document, filing or study referred to but not linked or quoted
- a number that conflicts with another one here or with something well known
- anything that should be present given the subject and is missing

For each lead, say what it is and what following it up might tell me. Do not
summarise. I only want the leads.

Chase the detail

What it does
Takes a specific claim from a weak source and tries to confirm it against a source that can actually stand behind it.
What it replicates
Treating a poor source as a lead rather than evidence. You do not cite the blog; you take the checkable fact out of it and find where it can be verified.
When to use it
Whenever something matters and its only source so far is weak.
Prompt
There is a detail here that needs verifying against a better source.

If it is clear from our chat which detail I mean, work with that. If not, ask me
which one, and where I saw it.

Treat the current source as a lead, not as evidence. Find a source that can
actually support the detail: a primary document, an official body, a
peer-reviewed source, or named specialist reporting. Search for the detail
itself, not the general topic.

Tell me one of three things:
- Confirmed, with the source and what kind of source it is.
- Contradicted, with the source.
- Could not confirm it after a genuine attempt.

Do not settle for another version of the same weak source repeating the claim.

Resolve and record claims

What it does
Keeps a running record of every claim that matters, its status, and its source, so nothing is silently asserted or silently dropped.
What it replicates
Tracking what you have actually established versus what you are still carrying on trust. It is the difference between a finding you can defend and one you hope holds up.
When to use it
At any point in a research task. If you start it partway through, it will build the record from what has been discussed so far.
Prompt
Keep a running record of every claim that matters to the conclusion.

Start from the claims already raised in this chat, then keep it updated as we go.
Present it as a table with these columns:
- Claim
- Does the conclusion depend on it? (yes / no)
- Status (confirmed / contradicted / unconfirmed)
- Best source so far, and what kind of source it is
- What was searched

When we reach conclusions, state as fact only the claims marked confirmed.
Anything unconfirmed must be flagged as such in the write-up, not stated plainly
and not left out.
Run just this method / Analyse

Put two patterns side by side

What it does
Lays two things against each other and reads the shape of the result, rather than just filling in cells.
What it replicates
A core analytical move. You learn something from a policy by setting it against a lifecycle, or from one product by setting it against another, that you cannot see by reading either alone. The insight is in the pattern that emerges.
When to use it
When you have two structured things and suspect the relationship between them will tell you something.
Prompt
Put two things side by side and tell me what the comparison reveals.

If it is clear from our chat what the two things are, use them. If only one is
clear, suggest a good thing to set it against. If neither is, ask me.

Lay them out in a table. Then, and this is the important part, read the shape of
the result rather than describing each cell. Tell me:
- where the elements spread out evenly, and where they bunch together
- where one side is crowded and the other is empty
- what those patterns imply

If most of one will not line up against the other, do not force it. Tell me they
do not fit well together, and suggest a better pairing.
Run just this method / Conclude

Look for evidence against the conclusion

What it does
Once there is a conclusion, actively searches for evidence that it is wrong and deals with the strongest thing it finds.
What it replicates
The habit that most separates careful researchers from confident ones: trying to break your own conclusion before relying on it. Ordinary searching finds more of what you already believe. This deliberately looks the other way.
When to use it
After reaching any conclusion that will inform a decision, and before writing it up as settled.
Prompt
Take the conclusion we have reached and try to disprove it.

Use the conclusion from our chat. If it is not clear which one I mean, ask.

Search for evidence that it is wrong, not more evidence that it is right. Word
the searches to find the opposite: if the conclusion is that something works,
search for reports of it failing, criticism of it, or cases where it was
overstated.

Then take the strongest thing you find and address it directly. If it changes
the conclusion, say how. If it does not, explain why. Do not give me a vague
&quot;there are other views&quot; hedge. I want the strongest actual objection and a real
response to it.
Run just this method / Conclude

Test the draft

What it does
Checks a finished piece of research against the standards and reports where it falls short.
What it replicates
The final read-through a careful researcher gives their own work, looking for claims that outran their evidence.
When to use it
On a finished draft, including one written without any of these steps. It catches problems, though it cannot go back and do checking that never happened.
Prompt
Check the draft against these standards and report every place it falls short.

Use the draft already in this chat. If there is none, ask me for it. Do not
rewrite it; just tell me what fails and where.

- Every stated fact traces to a source good enough to support it (a primary
  document, official body, peer-reviewed work, or named specialist reporting).
- Anything not confirmed is marked as unconfirmed, not stated as fact and not
  quietly left out.
- The conclusion names the specific claims it rests on, and each is confirmed.
- The strongest case against the conclusion is addressed, not avoided.

For each failure, quote the sentence and say which standard it misses.

Write the top post

What it does
Produces just the top post layer from a finished report or conclusion: the finding and why it matters.
What it replicates
The judgment of stating the single most relevant fact plainly and its relevance to the reader, resisting the urge to add context that belongs a layer down.
When to use it
When you have a report or conclusion you are happy with and want the one-glance version for a channel.
Prompt
Write the top post for this research, for an internal channel.

Use the report or conclusion already in this chat. If there is none, ask me for
it.

Two sentences. First, the specific finding stated as a fact, naming the actual
development rather than a category. Second, why it matters to the people who
will read it. Nothing else: no context, no detail, no third sentence. That is
the thread&#x27;s job.

Write the thread

What it does
Produces just the thread layer: the questions a reader has right after the finding.
What it replicates
Knowing which two or three questions a reader actually has next, and answering them without retelling the whole thing.
When to use it
When you have the finding settled and want the middle layer that earns the click to the full document.
Prompt
Write the thread for this research, the layer under the top post.

Use the report or conclusion already in this chat. If there is none, ask me for
it.

Answer the questions a reader has right after the finding: why now, what has
actually changed, and who should care. Short paragraphs, the load-bearing word
first in each. One comparison or piece of context if it earns its place. Do not
walk through the source material in order, and do not restate the top post or
pull in the full document&#x27;s depth.

Write the linked document

What it does
Produces just the full document layer: the depth, sourcing and limits for the reader who wants all of it.
What it replicates
Writing the full account for an engaged reader, conclusion first, with the evidence and its limits laid out honestly.
When to use it
When you want the depth layer written up on its own, for a wiki page or a longer document.
Prompt
Write the full document for this research, the layer people reach when they want
depth.

Use the report or conclusion already in this chat. If there is none, ask me for
it.

Lead with the finding, then give the evidence with its sourcing and how well
each claim is stood up, the second-order implications, and the limits and what
was not confirmed. Front-load the conclusion within each section rather than
building up to it. Plain declarative prose. Report only what was confirmed;
anything unconfirmed is named as such.
